Using LLMs with PhenotypeR

2026-10-20

Caveat emptor

Caveats

I will be showing you what you can do, not what you should do


Creating LLM tools for phenotyping is relatively easy. Creating tools that are trusted, improve the quality of research, and provide a good user experience is the hard part.


Choice of model undoubtedly matters. But not clear which model is best, by how much, or for which task.


System prompts and user input can also have a dramatic impact on the results.


Closed-weight cloud-based models show performance drift, making validation even harder.

More caveats

Reliance on LLM tooling in OHDSI can increase barrier to entry, especially if tools are tied to specific providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, etc)


And even more so if tools need access to other paid-for APIs (e.g. clinical resources)

Even more caveats

Low quality health research is prevalent


Reproducibility crisis in health research


LLM integration can go both ways. Increasing risk of OMOP-slop vs additional context and double-checking.

Working with LLMs from R

ellmer

We’ll be using the ellmer R package for interacting with LLMs.


Provides a simple interface for working with LLMs in a consistent manner.


Building on ellmer, we can add model-agnostic support with users choosing the model to use.

Integrating LLMs with PhenotypeR

Designed first for open-weight locally-hosted models

  • Available to all of the OHDSI community


  • Smoother package development (easy to implement, cheaper, and straightforward to test)


  • Easy to switch to using frontier models (and see if they are worth the extra cost).


  • But 6+ months behind “state-of-the-art”. (Although allows us to learn from experience of others)

Human-in-the-loop

  • Designed to support the human phenotyper, not automate them away.


  • All LLM-generated artefacts can be inspected, reviewed, and edited.


  • Main use of LLM is to provide additional context making it easier to double-check results

Phenotyping tasks

“A problem well stated is a problem half solved.”

Charles F. Kettering (1876 – 1958)


Nowadays, a problem well stated is a problem 95% solved.

Phenotyping tasks

Started with lower-stakes, human-in-the-loop, phenotyping tasks:

  • Drafting clinical descriptions
  • Reviewing codes included that led to cohort inclusion
  • Contextualising PhenotypeR results

Higher-risk tasks currently out-of-scope:

  • Searching for codes.
  • Creating cohort logic.
  • End-to-end phenotyping

Benchmarking LLM performance

We can use the vitals package to test how closely LLM output matches known clinical benchmarks.

library(vitals)
vitals::vitals_log_dir_set(tempdir())

age_benchmark <- tibble(
  input = "Summarise the clinical presentation and typical age at diagnosis of hypertension",
  target = "50"
)

score_within_5_years <- function(samples) {
  est_age <- purrr::map_int(samples$solver_metadata, "average_age_at_diagnosis")
  target_age <- as.integer(samples$target)
  
  list(
    score = factor(
      ifelse(abs(est_age - target_age) <= 5, "Pass", "Fail"),
      levels = c("Fail", "Pass"), 
      ordered = TRUE
    )
  )
}

chat_medgemma_fresh <- chat_medgemma$clone()
chat_medgemma_fresh$set_turns(list())
chat_medgemma_fresh$set_system_prompt(system_prompt)

age_task <- Task$new(
  dataset = age_benchmark,
  solver = generate_structured(
    solver_chat = chat_medgemma_fresh,
    type = type_object(
      clinical_summary = type_string(
        description = "Brief 1-sentence summary of the presentation."
      ),
      average_age_at_diagnosis = type_integer("Estimated average age in years")
    )
  ),

search_hecate_standard_concepts <- function(query) {
  req <- request("https://hecate.pantheon-hds.com/api/search_standard") |>
    req_url_query(q = query)
  resp <- req_perform(req)
  resp_body_string(resp)
}

According to the **National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) 
guideline NG136** (*Hypertension in adults: diagnosis and management*), the 
diagnosis of hypertension is a stepped process that relies on confirming 
elevated clinic readings with out-of-office measurements.

Here is a summary of the NICE approach to diagnosing hypertension:

---

### 1. Initial Clinic Blood Pressure Measurement
* **Preparation:** Ensure the patient is seated, relaxed, and in a quiet 
environment. Palpate the radial or brachial pulse before measuring (if the 
pulse is irregular, measure blood pressure manually, as automated devices may 
be inaccurate).
* **Both Arms:** At the first assessment, measure blood pressure in both arms. 
If the difference in readings between arms is more than **15 mmHg**, repeat the
measurements. If the difference remains more than 15 mmHg on the second 
measurement, use the arm with the **higher** reading for all subsequent 
measurements.
* **Multiple Readings:** If the first clinic blood pressure reading is **140/90
mmHg or higher**, take a second measurement during the consultation. If the 
second measurement is substantially different from the first, take a third 
measurement.
* **Recording:** Record the **lower** of the last two measurements as the 
clinic blood pressure.

---

### 2. Confirming the Diagnosis (ABPM or HBPM)
Do not diagnose hypertension based on a single clinic visit unless it is a 
hypertensive emergency. 

* **If Clinic BP is between 140/90 mmHg and 180/120 mmHg:**
  * **Gold Standard – ABPM (Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring):** Offer ABPM
to confirm the diagnosis. Ensure at least 2 measurements per hour are taken 
during the person’s usual waking hours (e.g., 08:00 to 22:00). Use the average 
value of at least **14 measurements** taken during waking hours to confirm the 
diagnosis.
  * **Alternative – HBPM (Home Blood Pressure Monitoring):** If the person is 
unable to tolerate ABPM, offer HBPM. The patient should take two consecutive 
measurements (at least 1 minute apart) while seated, twice daily (ideally 
morning and evening) for at least **4 days** (ideally 7 days). Discard the 
measurements taken on the first day and use the average value of all the 
remaining measurements to confirm the diagnosis.

* **If Clinic BP is 180/120 mmHg or higher (Severe Hypertension):**
  * **Refer for same-day specialist review** if there are signs of:
    * Retinal haemorrhage or papilloedema (accelerated/malignant hypertension).
    * Life-threatening symptoms (e.g., new-onset confusion, chest pain, signs 
of heart failure, or acute kidney injury).
    * Suspected phaeochromocytoma (e.g., labile or postural hypotension, 
headache, palpitations, pallor, diaphoresis).
  * **If no same-day referral is indicated:** Carry out investigations for 
target organ damage as soon as possible:
    * If target organ damage is identified, consider starting antihypertensive 
drug treatment immediately without waiting for ABPM/HBPM results.
    * If no target organ damage is identified, confirm diagnosis using 
ABPM/HBPM (or repeat clinic BP within 7 days).

---

### 3. Diagnostic Thresholds (Staging)

Hypertension is diagnosed and staged using **both** clinic and ABPM/HBPM 
averages:

| Stage | Clinic Blood Pressure | **AND** ABPM / HBPM Average |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stage 1 Hypertension** | 140/90 mmHg to 159/99 mmHg | **135/85 mmHg to 
149/94 mmHg** |
| **Stage 2 Hypertension** | 160/100 mmHg to 179/119 mmHg | **150/95 mmHg or 
higher** |
| **Stage 3 (Severe) Hypertension** | **Systolic ≥ 180 mmHg** OR **Diastolic ≥ 
120 mmHg** | *(Confirmed via clinical assessment/ABPM as above)* |

*(Note: If Clinic BP is ≥ 140/90 mmHg, but the ABPM/HBPM average is < 135/85 
mmHg, this is considered **White-Coat Hypertension**).*

---

### 4. Concurrent Assessments (While Waiting for Confirmation)
While awaiting ABPM or HBPM confirmation, NICE recommends completing two vital 
assessments for all patients with suspected hypertension:

1. **Cardiovascular Risk Assessment:** Use a formal risk assessment tool (such 
as **QRISK3**) to estimate the patient's 10-year risk of cardiovascular 
disease.
2. **Investigations for Target Organ Damage:**
   * **Urine:** Test for haematuria using a reagent strip, and send a urine 
sample to test for the albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR) to check for proteinuria.
   * **Bloods:** Measure electrolytes, creatinine, estimated glomerular 
filtration rate (eGFR), total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and HbA1c.
   * **Eyes:** Examine the fundi for the presence of hypertensive retinopathy.
   * **Heart:** Arrange a 12-lead electrocardiograph (ECG) to check for left 
ventricular hypertrophy or other cardiac abnormalities.

library(ragnar)
library(pdftools)
chunks <- pdf_text(nice_url) |>

store <- ragnar_store_create(

ragnar_store_insert(store, chunks)

According to the **NICE NG136** guideline (*Terms used in this guideline*), the
blood pressure thresholds that define Stage 1 and Stage 2 hypertension are:

### **Stage 1 Hypertension**
* **Clinic blood pressure:** Ranging from **140/90 mmHg to 159/99 mmHg**
* **AND subsequent ABPM daytime average or HBPM average:** Ranging from 
**135/85 mmHg to 149/94 mmHg**

---

### **Stage 2 Hypertension**
* **Clinic blood pressure:** **160/100 mmHg or higher**, but **less than 
180/120 mmHg**
* **AND subsequent ABPM daytime average or HBPM average:** **150/95 mmHg or 
higher**

*(Note: A clinic systolic blood pressure of 180 mmHg or higher or a clinic 
diastolic blood pressure of 120 mmHg or higher defines **Stage 3 or severe 
hypertension**.)*
